Project Objective

This project uses satellite imagery, weather variables, and a machine learning model to estimate potential wildfire spread across California. Our goal is to predict the spread of wildfires, specifically in California, and see how accurate the predictions are.

Data

For our data, we used CAL FIRE Historical Fire Perimeters. It contains 22,810 records, having a total of 21 columns. It gave us information about the containment date as well as the GIS calculated acres. We also used Zenodo's weather data. The weather dataset contains 14,988 entries with 14 columns.

Preprocessing the Data

For our data, we combined two datasets together. The wildfire dataset contains information about fire incidents including alarm date, containment date, fire name, and GIS calculated acres. The weather dataset has daily weather observations such as precipitation, average wind speed, and maximum temperature.

Correlation Matrix

We created a correlation matrix in order to see how strong the relationships are between the different features and the fire size. Based on the numbers, we can see that the correlation from weather features to fire sizes is not high. This means that there is no linear relationship between weather and fire sizes. This lets us know not to use a linear model.

This visualization gives us a better visual on the relationship between wind speed and fire size. It is a common belief that higher wind speed means the fire will be bigger. However, from our data, we can see that most of the fires are usually from when the wind speed is 6mph to 10 mph. Not only that, but from our graph, from 6 mph to 8 mph, the fire with the biggest size is between these two wind speeds.

This graph gives us more information if having days without rain would affect fire size in the case where the temperature goes high. It strengthen of the fire is more likely to start when the day has high temperature and is more dry after having no rain.

Analytical Methods

A technical overview of the datasets, model architecture, and training pipeline used to build the wildfire spread predictor.

Datasets

The NASA HLS Burn Scars dataset (Hugging Face) provides 804 labeled 512×512 satellite scenes from 2018–2021. Each scene has 6 spectral bands (Blue, Green, Red, NIR, SWIR1, SWIR2) and a pixel-level binary burn mask. Class split: 11% burned, 88% unburned, 1% no-data.

A California weather and fire CSV (CAL FIRE / NOAA, 1984–2025) provides daily max temperature, wind speed, lagged wind speed, temperature range, and a wind-to-temperature ratio. Stored in BigQuery and queried live on the Home page.

Model Architecture

The core model is Prithvi-EO-2.0 (300M params) — a geospatial foundation model with a ViT encoder and UNet decoder that produces per-pixel burn logits at 512×512 / 30m resolution.

A weather encoder MLP maps 5 weather scalars to a 2-class bias vector, which is added to Prithvi's spatial logits. This lets weather conditions globally shift burn vs. unburned predictions without changing the spatial feature extraction.

Training

Two-phase strategy: Phase 1 freezes the Prithvi backbone and trains only the weather encoder and decoder (~20 epochs). Phase 2 unfreezes all weights for end-to-end fine-tuning at a lower learning rate (~20 epochs).

CrossEntropy loss with class weights [1.0, 2.0] compensates for the imbalanced burn ratio. Trained on Google Colab with bf16 mixed precision.

Live Inference Pipeline

On prediction, the system queries NASA STAC CMR for the most recent cloud-free Sentinel-2 tile within a 180-day window. A 512×512 chip (~15×15 km) is streamed from NASA cloud-optimized GeoTIFFs via GDAL, normalized across 6 bands, and passed through the model with weather scalars.

Output: burn probability heatmap + binary mask. Predicted acres = burned pixels × 900 m² / 4047 m²/acre.

References: Prithvi-EO-2.0 (NASA/IBM, 2024) · HLS Burn Scars (nasa-impact, HuggingFace) · Terratorch (IBM Research) · PyTorch Lightning

Major Findings

Key results from our exploratory analysis and model predictions.

Wind Speed is Not the Dominant Driver

Contrary to the common assumption that higher wind speeds cause larger fires, our data shows that the majority of California wildfires — including the largest ones — occur at moderate wind speeds of 6–10 mph. Fires at extreme wind speeds were comparatively rare. This suggests that wind speed alone is a poor predictor of fire size, and that its interaction with temperature and moisture conditions matters more.

Heat + Drought Conditions Amplify Fire Risk

High maximum temperatures combined with extended dry periods (low lagged rainfall) consistently appear in the largest fire events. When temperatures exceed 90°F and rainfall has been absent for several days, fire size increases substantially. This interaction informed our decision to include both max temperature and lagged wind as features in the weather encoder.

Weather Features Show Moderate Correlation with Fire Size

The correlation matrix reveals that no single weather variable strongly predicts fire size in isolation. Max temperature and wind/temp ratio show the highest correlations, while raw wind speed alone has a weak relationship. This supported our choice to combine multiple weather variables into a learned embedding rather than relying on any one feature.

Satellite Imagery Captures Burn Boundaries

The Prithvi-EO-2.0 model, trained on NASA HLS burn scars, successfully identifies burned vs. unburned regions from spectral signatures — particularly the SWIR bands, which are highly sensitive to char and vegetation loss. The predicted burn masks visually align with expected fire perimeters at 30m resolution.

Weather Conditioning Shifts Predictions

Adding the weather encoder meaningfully changes the model's output. Under high-temperature, low-wind conditions the model predicts smaller, more contained burn areas. Under the 6–10 mph wind range with high temperatures, predicted acreage increases noticeably — consistent with the patterns observed in historical fire data.

Cloud Cover Limits Live Tile Availability

California's coastal and northern regions often lack recent cloud-free Sentinel-2 tiles, requiring the system to look back up to 180 days. Inland and southern California locations tend to have more recent imagery available due to lower cloud cover, resulting in faster and more accurate predictions for those areas.

How It Works

A plain-language guide to what FIRECAST does, what you put in, and what the results mean.

What Is This?

FIRECAST is a wildfire burn area predictor for California. It combines real satellite imagery from NASA with weather conditions you control to estimate how much land could burn if a fire started at a chosen location.

Inputs

1. Location — Click anywhere on the California map in the Fire Spread Predictor tab. The tool fetches the most recent cloud-free satellite image of that area (up to 180 days back).

2. Weather conditions — Five sliders let you set the conditions:

  • Max Temperature — The day's peak temperature (°F)
  • Wind Speed — Current wind speed (mph)
  • Temp Range — The swing between the daily high and low temperatures (°F)
  • Lagged Wind — Wind speed from prior days, reflecting how conditions have been building
  • Wind / Temp Ratio — A combined measure of wind and heat stress

Outputs

After clicking Run Prediction, you get three images side by side and three metrics:

Input RGB

True-color satellite photo of your selected area, streamed live from NASA.

Predicted Mask

Pixels the model thinks would burn, highlighted in orange.

Burn Probability

A heatmap — brighter areas have a higher predicted chance of burning.

Predicted Burn

Estimated total area that could burn, in acres.

Peak Burn Probability

The single highest-confidence pixel in the scene, shown as a percentage.

Tile Date

The date the satellite image was originally captured by Sentinel-2.

The Fire Risk Index gauge updates in real time as you adjust the sliders, even before running a prediction.

Limitations

  • Only works within California.
  • Best suited for small to medium fires. Our training dataset (CAL FIRE 1984–2025) is dominated by small and medium fire events, so the model was intentionally tuned to perform well in those cases. Predictions for very large fire events may be less reliable.
  • Requires a recent cloud-free satellite image — coastal and northern areas may use imagery up to 6 months old due to higher cloud cover.
  • Predictions reflect potential burn conditions under the weather inputs you set, not active fire tracking or real-time fire progression.
